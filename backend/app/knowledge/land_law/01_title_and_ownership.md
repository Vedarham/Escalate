# Title and ownership

## TT-01 | What "clear and marketable title" means
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: title, ownership, marketable title, due diligence
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.54, s.55)

A seller can only transfer the rights they actually hold. A clear and marketable title means the seller is the absolute owner, can transfer without anyone else's consent, and the property is free from defects, encumbrances, claims and litigation that a prudent buyer would object to. Under s.55 of the Transfer of Property Act the seller must disclose material defects in the property or title that the buyer could not discover with ordinary care, and must produce the title documents for inspection. In practice the buyer has to prove title through a chain of registered documents, revenue records and searches, usually verified by an advocate's title report.

## TT-02 | Title search: how far back and what to examine
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: title search, chain of title, mother deed, due diligence, 30 years
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882; Registration Act, 1908; Limitation Act, 1963

A title search traces ownership of the property backwards through every transfer (sale, gift, partition, inheritance, release, court decree). Banks and advocates commonly examine at least 30 years of history, the period linked to the longest limitation periods for property claims, and longer where the chain is unclear. The search covers: the "mother deed" or original root of title; each subsequent registered deed; inheritance documents for every death in the chain; revenue records (mutation entries, record of rights); encumbrance searches at the Sub-Registrar office; and litigation searches. Every break in the chain (a missing deed, an unrecorded heir, an unregistered transfer) is a title defect that must be cured before purchase.

## TT-03 | Sale deed vs agreement to sell
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: sale deed, agreement to sell, conveyance, ownership transfer, registration
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.54); Registration Act, 1908 (s.17)

Under s.54 of the Transfer of Property Act, ownership of immovable property worth ₹100 or more passes only by a registered sale deed (conveyance). An agreement to sell is only a contract to sell in future. It does not by itself create any ownership interest in the property, though it gives the buyer a right to sue for specific performance if the seller backs out. Until the sale deed is registered, the buyer is not the owner, even if they have paid most of the price or hold an agreement and possession.

## TT-04 | Compulsory registration and the effect of non-registration
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: registration, unregistered document, sub-registrar, time limit, stamp duty
sources: Registration Act, 1908 (s.17, s.23, s.49)

Section 17 of the Registration Act makes registration compulsory for non-testamentary documents that create, transfer or extinguish rights in immovable property worth ₹100 or more (sale deeds, gift deeds, mortgages other than by deposit of title deeds, releases), and for leases of more than one year. Under s.23 a document must normally be presented for registration within four months of execution. Under s.49 an unregistered document that requires registration does not affect the property and cannot be received as evidence of the transaction. A limited exception allows it as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance or as evidence of a collateral transaction. Registration also requires the correct stamp duty to be paid.

## TT-05 | Mutation and revenue records are not proof of title
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: mutation, revenue records, khata, patta, 7/12, ownership proof
sources: Supreme Court: Suraj Bhan v. Financial Commissioner (2007) 6 SCC 186; Jitendra Singh v. State of M.P. (2021)

Mutation means updating revenue or municipal records (7/12 extract, khata, patta, khatauni, property card) in the new owner's name, mainly so that land revenue or property tax is collected from the right person. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that mutation entries are for fiscal purposes and do not by themselves create or extinguish title. Ownership is decided by registered title documents and, if disputed, by a civil court. Even so, buyers should insist that revenue records match the title chain. A mismatch (a different name, area or survey number) is a red flag that must be explained and corrected. After buying, the buyer must apply for mutation in their own name.

## TT-06 | Encumbrance certificate (EC) and its limits
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: encumbrance certificate, EC, form 15, form 16, mortgage, search
sources: Registration Act, 1908; state registration rules

An encumbrance certificate is issued by the Sub-Registrar and lists registered transactions affecting a property (sales, mortgages, leases, releases) for a chosen period. In states such as Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, Form 15 lists the transactions and Form 16 is a "nil encumbrance" certificate. Obtain an EC for at least 13–30 years and match every entry with the title chain. Limitations: an EC shows only documents registered at that registry, so it will not show unregistered agreements, equitable mortgages by deposit of title deeds (unless the state requires them to be noted), court attachments or pending suits. Combine it with CERSAI, litigation and revenue searches.

## TT-07 | Possession, physical inspection and measurement
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: possession, site inspection, survey, measurement, boundaries, occupants
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.55); general due diligence practice

Always inspect the land in person and have a licensed surveyor measure it against the survey or sub-division numbers and the village or city survey map (FMB sketch, tippan, city survey map). Check that boundaries, area, access road and neighbours match the title documents. Ask who is in possession. Tenants, licensees, relatives, caretakers or encroachers in occupation may have rights that survive the sale, and a buyer is treated as having notice of the rights of a person in actual possession. Get vacant physical possession at registration or as the deed specifies, and record the handover in a possession letter or receipt.

## TT-08 | Ostensible owner and bona fide purchaser protection
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: ostensible owner, bona fide purchaser, good faith, reasonable care
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.41)

Section 41 of the Transfer of Property Act protects a buyer who purchases from a person who appears to be the owner (an "ostensible owner") with the real owner's express or implied consent, if the buyer paid consideration and acted in good faith after taking reasonable care to check that the transferor had power to transfer. Courts interpret "reasonable care" strictly: a buyer who skipped title searches, ignored a mismatch in records or did not ask about occupants in possession may lose this protection. Thorough, documented due diligence is therefore also legal protection.

## TT-09 | Survey number, sub-division and area mismatches
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: survey number, sub-division, area mismatch, hissa, boundaries, measurement
sources: State land revenue codes; general practice

A frequent problem is that the sale deed, revenue record and physical land disagree. Examples: the deed mentions survey no. 45/2 but the 7/12 or RTC shows 45/2A; the area in the deed exceeds the record of rights; or the plot on the ground is in a different sub-division. Causes include unrecorded sub-division (hissa or phodi), old measurements, or deliberate misdescription. Before buying, get a fresh survey from the land records department, demand that the seller corrects the records (rectification or phodi or sub-division proceedings), and describe the property in the sale deed by survey number, area, boundaries on all four sides and a sketch.
