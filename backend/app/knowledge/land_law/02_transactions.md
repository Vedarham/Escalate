# Transactions, payments and taxes

## TX-01 | Earnest money, advance and forfeiture
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: earnest money, advance, token amount, forfeiture, refund, agreement to sell
sources: Indian Contract Act, 1872 (s.74); Satish Batra v. Sudhir Rawal (2013) 1 SCC 345

Pay a token or earnest amount only after preliminary document checks, and only against a written agreement that states the price, payment schedule, the documents the seller must produce, a deadline for completion, and what happens to the money if either side defaults. Courts allow forfeiture of a genuine earnest-money deposit when the buyer defaults, if the agreement provides for it. A large "advance" that is really part payment can generally be forfeited only to the extent of actual loss. Pay by traceable banking channels, never in cash, and keep receipts.

## TX-02 | Part performance protection (Section 53A)
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: part performance, 53A, possession, agreement to sell, registered agreement
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.53A); Registration Act, 1908 (s.17(1A)), amended 2001

A buyer who has taken possession under a written agreement and is willing to perform their side can use s.53A of the Transfer of Property Act as a shield to protect that possession against the seller. Since the 2001 amendment to the Registration Act (s.17(1A)), the agreement must be registered for this protection. Section 53A is a defence, not a mode of acquiring title. The buyer still needs a registered sale deed, or a court decree for specific performance, to become owner.

## TX-03 | Specific performance when the seller backs out
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: specific performance, breach, seller refuses, limitation, suit
sources: Specific Relief Act, 1963 (as amended 2018); Limitation Act, 1963 (Art. 54)

If the seller refuses to execute the sale deed after an agreement to sell, the buyer can file a civil suit for specific performance. Since the 2018 amendment, specific performance is generally enforced as a rule rather than being discretionary, subject to the statutory exceptions. The buyer must show readiness and willingness to perform (for example, funds available for the balance price). The suit must be filed within 3 years from the date fixed for performance or, if no date was fixed, from when the buyer learns the seller has refused (Limitation Act, Art. 54). Registering a lis pendens or caveat where available, and acting quickly, helps prevent the seller from selling to someone else.

## TX-04 | Power of attorney sales and "GPA sales"
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: power of attorney, GPA, SPA, GPA sale, agent, Suraj Lamp
sources: Suraj Lamp & Industries v. State of Haryana (2012) 1 SCC 656; Powers of Attorney Act, 1882; Registration Act, 1908

A power of attorney (PoA) only authorises an agent to act for the owner; it does not transfer ownership. In Suraj Lamp & Industries the Supreme Court held that "SA/GPA/Will" transactions (an agreement, a general power of attorney and a will used instead of a registered sale deed) do not convey title, and that immovable property can be transferred only by a registered deed of conveyance. If buying through a PoA holder, verify that: the PoA is registered or properly authenticated; it specifically authorises sale of this property; the principal is alive (a PoA ends on the principal's death) and has not revoked it; and preferably the owner confirms the sale directly. A chain of PoA transfers with no registered sale deeds is a serious title defect.

## TX-05 | Lis pendens: buying property under litigation
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: lis pendens, pending litigation, court case, suit, s.52, litigation search
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.52)

Under the doctrine of lis pendens (s.52 of the Transfer of Property Act), a property that is the subject of a pending suit in which a right to it is directly in question cannot be transferred so as to affect the rights of any party to that suit. A buyer during the case takes the property subject to the final decree, even without knowledge of the case. Some states additionally require a notice of lis pendens to be registered. Before buying, run litigation searches (district and high court case status portals, revenue courts, and the Sub-Registrar), ask for a declaration of no litigation in the sale deed, and avoid properties with pending title or partition suits.

## TX-06 | Stamp duty, registration fee and guideline value
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: stamp duty, registration fee, ready reckoner, circle rate, guideline value, undervaluation
sources: Indian Stamp Act, 1899 and state stamp acts; Registration Act, 1908

Stamp duty is a state levy and varies by state, area and sometimes buyer category. It is typically in the range of about 4–8% of the higher of the agreed price and the government's minimum value. Some states offer concessions for women buyers. The registration fee is commonly about 1% (often capped, for example ₹30,000 in Maharashtra). The minimum value is called the ready reckoner rate (Maharashtra), guideline value (Karnataka, Tamil Nadu), or circle rate (Delhi, Uttar Pradesh). Under-stamped documents can be impounded and attract penalties, and under-valuing the deed to save duty also creates income-tax exposure (see TX-07). Check the current rate on the state registration (IGR) portal.

## TX-07 | Income tax: TDS on purchase and stamp-value rules
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: TDS, 194-IA, 26QB, capital gains, 50C, 56(2)(x), NRI seller, tax
sources: Income-tax Act, 1961 (s.194-IA, s.195, s.50C, s.56(2)(x))

If the consideration for immovable property (other than rural agricultural land) is ₹50 lakh or more, the buyer must deduct TDS at 1% under s.194-IA, deposit it using Form 26QB, and give the seller Form 16B. Since 2022 the 1% applies to the higher of the consideration and the stamp-duty value. If the seller is a non-resident, s.195 applies instead: TDS at much higher rates on the capital gain, and the buyer needs a TAN. If the stamp-duty value exceeds the agreed price by more than the permitted tolerance (currently 10%), the difference can be taxed in the buyer's hands (s.56(2)(x)) and the stamp value is deemed the seller's sale consideration (s.50C). Take professional tax advice for large or NRI transactions.

## TX-08 | Paying for property: banking channels and cash limits
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: payment, cash, bank transfer, 269SS, 269ST, black money, receipts
sources: Income-tax Act, 1961 (s.269SS, s.269ST, s.271D)

Pay only through banking channels (cheque, RTGS or NEFT, demand draft) to the account of the registered owner or owners, and record the payment details in the sale deed. Accepting ₹20,000 or more in cash as an advance or for a property transaction attracts penalties under the Income-tax Act (s.269SS read with s.271D). Cash payments also leave the buyer without proof if a dispute arises. Where there are several co-owners, pay each according to their share, or obtain a written direction from all of them.

## TX-09 | RERA: buying a plot or flat in a project
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: RERA, builder, project, registration, delay, refund, plotted development, apartment
sources: Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (ss.3, 13, 14, 18, 31)

Real estate projects on more than 500 sq m of land or with more than 8 units (including plotted developments) must be registered with the state RERA authority before being advertised or sold (s.3). Check the project's RERA registration, approvals, completion date and litigation on the RERA website. The promoter cannot take more than 10% of the price as advance before a registered agreement for sale (s.13), and cannot change approved plans without the consent required by s.14. If possession is delayed, the buyer can withdraw with a refund plus interest, or stay on and claim interest for the delay (s.18). Complaints are filed with the RERA authority (s.31). Buying a plot in an unregistered "project" that should have been registered is a red flag.

## TX-10 | Joint ownership and co-owners' consent
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: co-owner, joint owners, undivided share, consent, all owners must sign
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.44); general practice

When a property stands in the names of several people (co-owners, joint holders, heirs), all of them must sign the sale deed, personally or through valid powers of attorney. A co-owner can sell only their own undivided share. A buyer of an undivided share steps into that co-owner's shoes, may have to sue for partition to get a specific portion, and in the case of a dwelling house belonging to an undivided family may not be entitled to joint possession (s.44 TPA). If one co-owner refuses or cannot be traced, do not buy the whole property on the strength of the others' signatures.
