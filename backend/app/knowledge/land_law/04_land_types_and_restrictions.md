# Land types and legal restrictions

## LR-01 | Who can buy agricultural land
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: agricultural land, farmer, agriculturist, non-agriculturist, restriction, tenancy act
sources: State land reform and tenancy laws (e.g. Maharashtra Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1948 s.63; Gujarat Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1948 s.63; Karnataka Land Reforms Act, 1961 as amended 2020)

Agricultural land is a state subject, and many states restrict who may buy it. In Maharashtra and Gujarat, transfers of agricultural land to a non-agriculturist generally require the Collector's permission under s.63 of the respective Tenancy Acts, so a buyer usually needs proof that they are an agriculturist (for example, holding agricultural land in the state). Karnataka removed its ss.79A–79C restrictions in 2020, allowing non-agriculturists to buy, though ceiling limits still apply. Other states have their own rules and ceiling limits. Buying agricultural land in breach of these rules can lead to the sale being declared invalid and the land vesting in the government. Always verify eligibility under the specific state law before paying.

## LR-02 | Non-agricultural (NA) conversion and land use
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: NA conversion, non-agricultural, land use, conversion order, DC conversion, zoning
sources: State land revenue codes (e.g. Maharashtra Land Revenue Code, 1966 ss.42–44; Karnataka Land Revenue Act, 1964 s.95; U.P. Revenue Code, 2006 s.80)

Agricultural land cannot lawfully be used for houses, layouts or commercial buildings until its use is converted. The order is variously called NA permission, DC conversion or a declaration of non-agricultural use, and is issued under the state land revenue code, in line with the development plan or master-plan zoning. Before buying a "plot" carved out of farmland, verify: the conversion order and its conditions; that the conversion covers the exact survey and sub-division numbers; zoning under the development plan; the approved layout from the planning authority; and payment of conversion charges. Plots in unconverted or unapproved layouts may not get building permission, utility connections or bank loans, and may face demolition or regularisation fees.

## LR-03 | Government, assigned and inalienable land
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: government land, assigned land, granted land, inalienable, gairan, poramboke, PTCL
sources: State land grant rules; Karnataka SC/ST (Prohibition of Transfer of Certain Lands) Act, 1978; Limitation Act, 1963 (Art. 112)

Land granted or assigned by the government to landless persons, ex-servicemen, or Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe beneficiaries is often non-transferable, either permanently or for a fixed period, or transferable only with government permission (for example, Karnataka's PTCL Act). Common land such as grazing land (gairan or gomal), poramboke, tank beds and roads belongs to the government and cannot be bought from private persons. Transfers in breach of these rules are void and the land can be resumed even years later; the government has 30 years to sue for recovery (Limitation Act, Art. 112). Check the record of rights for words such as "granted", "assigned", "inam", "restricted tenure", "new tenure" or "Class II occupant".

## LR-04 | Tribal land and Scheduled Areas
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: tribal land, adivasi, scheduled area, fifth schedule, PESA, restriction
sources: Constitution of India, Fifth Schedule; state laws (e.g. Maharashtra Land Revenue Code, 1966 s.36A); PESA Act, 1996

Most states with tribal populations prohibit or strictly control transfer of land held by Scheduled Tribe members to non-tribals. For example, Maharashtra's Land Revenue Code s.36A requires the Collector's prior sanction, and Scheduled Area regulations under the Fifth Schedule apply. Such transfers made without permission are void, and the land can be restored to the tribal owner. In Scheduled Areas the gram sabha also has a role under PESA. Check the caste or tribe status of the land holder and any "tribal" or restricted entry in the record of rights.

## LR-05 | Land under acquisition, road widening or reservation
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: land acquisition, reservation, road widening, development plan, notification, RFCTLARR
sources: Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (s.11); state town planning acts

Once a preliminary notification for acquisition is issued (s.11 of the 2013 Land Acquisition Act), transactions in that land are restricted without the Collector's permission, and a buyer gets only the right to compensation, not the land. Development plans may also reserve land for roads, parks, schools or public purposes, or mark it for road widening, which limits construction. Before buying, obtain a zone certificate or part-plan from the planning authority, check land acquisition notifications for the village, and look for reservations or road lines crossing the plot.

## LR-06 | Forest, coastal and environmentally restricted land
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: forest land, CRZ, coastal, eco-sensitive zone, wetland, environmental clearance
sources: Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 1980 (formerly Forest (Conservation) Act); CRZ Notification, 2019; Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

Land recorded as forest, deemed forest, or within an eco-sensitive zone, wetland or Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) is subject to severe restrictions on non-forest use and construction. A private title does not override these restrictions. Check the record of rights and forest records for any "forest", "private forest" or "deemed forest" entry, and check the CRZ and eco-sensitive-zone maps for land near the coast, rivers, lakes or protected areas, before buying for development.

## LR-07 | NRI, OCI and foreign buyers
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: NRI, OCI, foreign national, FEMA, agricultural land, NRE, NRO, repatriation
sources: Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019; Income-tax Act, 1961 (s.195)

Under FEMA's Non-debt Instruments Rules, NRIs and OCI cardholders may buy residential and commercial immovable property in India, but not agricultural land, plantation property or farm houses (these can only be inherited, or acquired with RBI approval). Payment must come through normal banking channels: inward remittance, or NRE, NRO or FCNR accounts. Foreign nationals not of Indian origin who live outside India generally cannot buy property in India, except by inheritance or with approval, and citizens of certain neighbouring countries face additional restrictions. When an NRI is the seller, the buyer must deduct TDS under s.195 (see TX-07).

## LR-08 | Leasehold land (development authority, industrial and Gramthan)
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: leasehold, freehold, DDA, MIDC, industrial plot, lessor consent, transfer fee
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (ss.105–108); lease deeds of development authorities

Many urban and industrial plots (for example DDA, MIDC, CIDCO, GIDC, HUDA or HSVP, KIADB) are leasehold. The occupant holds a lease, often 60–99 years, and the authority remains owner. Transfers usually need the authority's prior permission or NOC, payment of transfer or unearned-increase charges, and compliance with lease conditions on use and construction deadlines. Some authorities allow conversion to freehold for a fee. Check the original lease, all transfer permissions, dues certificates and any breach notices before buying. Buying without the lessor's consent can lead to lease cancellation.

## LR-09 | Trust, Wakf, temple and society-owned land
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: trust property, wakf, temple land, devasthan, charity commissioner, endowment
sources: Waqf Act, 1995 (as amended); state public trust and religious endowment acts (e.g. Maharashtra Public Trusts Act, 1950 s.36)

Property belonging to a public trust, religious endowment, temple (devasthan or inam) or Wakf is subject to statutory protection. Sale or long lease generally requires prior sanction of the Charity Commissioner, endowment department or Waqf Board, and transfers without it can be void. Watch for entries such as "devasthan inam" or "waqf" in revenue records, or a trust or society as a past owner in the title chain. Ask for the sanction order and check that its conditions (price, purpose, auction) were met.
