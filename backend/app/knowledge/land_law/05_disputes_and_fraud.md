# Disputes, fraud and encumbrances

## DF-01 | Common land-purchase frauds and red flags
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: fraud, forgery, impersonation, double sale, fake documents, red flags, scam
sources: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (cheating and forgery offences; formerly IPC ss.420, 467, 468, 471); Specific Relief Act, 1963 (s.31)

Frequent frauds include: impersonation of the real owner (often an absentee, elderly or NRI owner); forged sale deeds or wills; the same property sold to two buyers (double sale); sale by one heir who hides the others; sale of government or assigned land; sale of mortgaged property; and fake approvals for layouts. Red flags: a price far below market; pressure to pay quickly or in cash; the seller refuses to give original documents or to meet at the property; recent rapid transfers or PoA chains; a mismatch between ID documents and revenue records; tenants or neighbours naming a different owner. Verify identity with original ID, photographs, biometric records at registration and neighbours' confirmation, and compare original deeds with certified copies from the Sub-Registrar.

## DF-02 | Remedies if you were defrauded
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: fraud remedy, cancellation of sale deed, police complaint, FIR, civil suit, recovery
sources: Specific Relief Act, 1963 (s.31); Limitation Act, 1963 (Art. 59); Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023

Civil remedies include a suit for cancellation of a void or voidable instrument (s.31 of the Specific Relief Act), generally within 3 years of learning the facts (Limitation Act, Art. 59); a suit for declaration of title and possession; and recovery of money paid with interest. Interim injunctions can stop further transfer or construction. Criminal remedies, such as a complaint or FIR for cheating, forgery and criminal breach of trust, run in parallel but do not by themselves cancel the deed. Act quickly: preserve payment records, documents and communications, lodge objections with the Sub-Registrar and revenue authorities to block mutation, and consult a litigation lawyer.

## DF-03 | Mortgaged property, bank charges and CERSAI search
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: mortgage, loan, bank, charge, CERSAI, NOC, equitable mortgage, title deeds with bank
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (s.58); SARFAESI Act, 2002; Registration Act, 1908

A property may be mortgaged to a bank, often by deposit of title deeds (an equitable mortgage), which may not appear in an encumbrance certificate. Some states, such as Maharashtra, require lenders to file notices of such mortgages with the Sub-Registrar. Search the CERSAI registry (Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest) for security interests created by banks and NBFCs, and ask whether the seller has the original title deeds. If the property is mortgaged, close the deal so that the loan is repaid directly to the bank from the sale proceeds, against a loan closure letter, release of the charge (including the CERSAI satisfaction) and return of the original deeds. A buyer of mortgaged property takes it subject to the mortgage.

## DF-04 | Adverse possession and long-standing occupants
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: adverse possession, encroachment, occupant, 12 years, limitation, possession claim
sources: Limitation Act, 1963 (Arts. 65, 112); Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur (2019) 8 SCC 729

A person in open, continuous, hostile possession of private land for 12 years, counted from when the possession became adverse to the owner's knowledge, can acquire title by adverse possession. The owner's suit for possession becomes time-barred (Limitation Act, Art. 65). The period is 30 years against government land (Art. 112). The Supreme Court held in Ravinder Kaur Grewal (2019) that a person who has perfected title by adverse possession can also sue to protect it. For a buyer this means: an occupant on vacant or neglected land may have an enforceable claim, and absentee owners must periodically assert possession. Never buy land occupied by third parties unless they vacate or their rights are settled.

## DF-05 | Encroachment and boundary disputes
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: encroachment, boundary dispute, neighbour, survey, demarcation, fencing
sources: State land revenue codes (boundary demarcation provisions); Specific Relief Act, 1963

Boundary disputes arise when fences or walls do not match survey boundaries, or when neighbours encroach. Before buying, get a joint measurement or demarcation by the government land records or survey office, with notice to adjoining owners, and compare the result with the survey map and title documents. If an encroachment is found, the seller should resolve it (by removal, settlement or a revised price) before registration. After purchase, fence the land and keep the demarcation record. Remedies include revenue demarcation proceedings and civil suits for possession and injunction.

## DF-06 | Easements and access rights (right of way)
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: easement, right of way, access road, landlocked plot, prescription, necessity
sources: Indian Easements Act, 1882 (ss.13, 15)

Check that the land has legal access to a public road. A plot reachable only across someone else's land depends on an easement. An easement of necessity arises on division of property (s.13 Indian Easements Act), and an easement by prescription arises after 20 years of peaceable, open, uninterrupted use as of right (s.15). Prefer a registered easement or right-of-way deed that is mentioned in the sale deed. Also check whether other people have easements over the land you are buying, such as pathways, drains, water channels or power lines, which will limit construction.

## DF-07 | Benami transactions
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: benami, proxy owner, name lending, benami property, confiscation
sources: Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988 (as amended 2016)

A benami transaction is one where property is held in one person's name but paid for by another for the latter's benefit, outside the recognised exceptions such as property in a spouse's or child's name paid from known sources, or property held by a fiduciary. The Benami Act (as amended in 2016) allows confiscation of benami property and prescribes penalties, and the real owner cannot sue to recover it from the benamidar. Buyers should avoid "name-lending" arrangements, and be careful when buying from someone who appears to be holding property for another person.

## DF-08 | Tenants and occupants in possession
jurisdiction: all-india
topics: tenant, occupant, rent control, eviction, lease, licensee, protected tenant
sources: Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (ss.105–111); state rent control and tenancy laws

A sale does not automatically end an existing tenancy. The buyer steps into the landlord's shoes and is bound by valid leases. Tenants protected under state rent control laws, or agricultural tenants under tenancy laws (who may have statutory purchase rights), can be very difficult to evict. Leases of more than one year must be registered. Before buying, identify every occupant, inspect any lease or leave-and-licence agreements, and either obtain vacant possession or price in the tenancy.
